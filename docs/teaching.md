# Teaching with Insonate

Insonate is for the first hours of abdominal ultrasound: probe handling, anatomy and knobology. It needs no hardware beyond a laptop. A webcam and a printed marker are optional.

## Learning goals

By the end of the ten guided tasks, a student should be able to:

1. **Handle the probe.** Know the probe marker convention, and slide, rotate, fan and rock on purpose. Recognise that a small rotation often fixes a view that a big slide does not.
2. **Find the standard abdominal views.** Kidneys in long and short axis, Morison's pouch, gallbladder, aorta, IVC, spleen, bladder and pancreas.
3. **Use acoustic windows.** Go through the liver and spleen, between ribs, and through a full bladder. Ask for a held breath to bring organs out from under the ribs.
4. **Recognise artefacts and explain them:** the clean shadow behind ribs, the "dirty" shadow behind gas, enhancement behind fluid, and lost contact.
5. **Set the machine:** depth to fit the organ, focus at the region of interest, gain and TGC for an evenly bright image, and frequency to trade resolution against penetration.
6. **Measure without foreshortening.** Find the longest plane before measuring a length.
7. **Use Doppler correctly:** colour flow direction (red toward, blue away), why colour vanishes at 90°, aliasing and the scale, the spectral waveforms of an artery and a vein, and angle correction under 60°.
8. **Recognise common findings:** gallstones, a polyp, acute cholecystitis, fatty liver and its grade, a liver cyst, a haemangioma, metastases, hydronephrosis and its grade, a kidney stone, a renal cyst, an abdominal aortic aneurysm, free fluid, splenomegaly and a pleural effusion.
9. **Write the report:** organ by organ on a structured template, with the measurements, then an impression that answers the clinical question, names each finding and its side, and calls a normal study normal.

## Handling the probe

Students can hold the probe in several ways, and mix them:

- **Drag the image itself.** The anatomy under the pointer follows the hand, as if the student slid the probe to bring it there; dragging up or down sweeps through it. Where sliding cannot bring a deep structure across (under the ribs, a deep point pivots with the probe), the hand heel–toes instead, which is worth pointing out. <kbd>Shift</kbd>- or right-drag rotates and rocks, the wheel fans, and a double-click brings a structure to the middle of the image.
- **The 3D body.** Grab the probe and move it from where it is; the wheel over it rotates it. Click elsewhere on the skin to put the probe there.
- **Keys or the pad.** <kbd>W</kbd> <kbd>A</kbd> <kbd>S</kbd> <kbd>D</kbd> slide, <kbd>Q</kbd> <kbd>E</kbd> rotate, <kbd>R</kbd> <kbd>F</kbd> fan, <kbd>Z</kbd> <kbd>C</kbd> rock. A tap nudges, a hold glides and speeds up after a second, and <kbd>Shift</kbd> gives fine control.
- **Hold <kbd>X</kbd>, or the pad's Press key, to press in.** Graded compression: the face sinks into the wall and pushes bowel gas aside, opening a window to the aorta or the pancreas. It is also how sonographic Murphy's sign is elicited: the patient winces only when the probe presses over an inflamed gallbladder.

## The ten tasks

| # | Task | Probe position | What to learn |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Right kidney, long axis | Right flank, longitudinal | Liver as a window; rib shadows; measure the length (normal 10–12 cm) |
| 2 | Right kidney, transverse | Rotate 90° from task 1 | Rotation as the key move; fan through the kidney |
| 3 | Morison's pouch | Right flank, longitudinal | The first FAST view: where free fluid collects |
| 4 | Gallbladder, long axis | Right subcostal, oblique | Bile is anechoic; posterior enhancement |
| 5 | Aorta, transverse | Epigastric, transverse | Landmarks (vertebra, IVC); bowel gas; measure the AP diameter (under 3 cm) |
| 6 | IVC, longitudinal | Right paramedian, longitudinal | Fanning to find a tube |
| 7 | Spleen, long axis | Left posterior axillary, intercostal | A high, posterior organ; breathing out clears the lung |
| 8 | Left kidney, long axis | Left flank, posterior | Harder than the right; the spleen as a window |
| 9 | Bladder, transverse | Suprapubic | Angling into the pelvis; enhancement |
| 10 | Pancreas, transverse | Epigastric, oblique | The splenic vein as the landmark; gas |

Each task has a goal, step-by-step guidance, a tip and a live score. The checklist under the score shows which requirement is missing: centred, aligned, in view, clear window or contact.

## Teaching cases and the mystery patient

The **Cases** tab turns the same patient into a teaching case: one abnormality is added to the tissue model, so every sign (a stone's shadow, a cyst's enhancement, the stripe of free fluid) comes from the same physics as the normal anatomy. Each case has a short history, what to look for, why it matters, and *Show me the finding*.

The **mystery patient** picks a case at random, normal included, and hides it. The student scans, chooses a diagnosis from the list, and gets the answer with its explanation. An instructor can send a class a mystery link for a chosen case (*Copy a mystery-patient link* on the case): the diagnosis is not readable in the link.

## The reporting exam

The **Report** tab is an exam: a patient arrives with a request form, the student scans and writes the report, and the report is marked against what the patient really has. Every case is new, unless a teacher gives an exam code.

### Levels

| Level | The case | What is marked |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 · Focused | One clear finding (a 15 mm stone, moderate hydronephrosis, a 5 cm aneurysm) in the organ the request asks about | That organ only: the right finding |
| 2 · Full survey | Usually one or two findings, now and then an incidental one; about one patient in eight is normal (fewer for the man of 67, whose own aneurysm is sometimes left in) | Every organ: each finding, its size, and the routine measurements (kidney and spleen lengths, aortic diameter) within ±20 % |
| 3 · Consultant | Up to three findings, often subtle (a 4 mm stone, mild hydronephrosis, grade 1 fatty liver, a little free fluid); incidental findings are common, the history may point elsewhere, and about one patient in eight is normal | As level 2, with grades, measurements within ±12 %, Murphy's sign, the wall in cholecystitis, and a written impression |

### Where the cases come from

A case starts from a clinical scenario: why the patient was sent, by whom, and what they have. Examples:

- biliary colic with gallstones (sometimes with a fatty liver);
- acute cholecystitis;
- dyspepsia with a polyp;
- raised liver enzymes with a fatty liver;
- a liver lesion to characterise;
- cancer staging with metastases;
- renal colic with an obstructing stone and hydronephrosis, or a non-obstructing stone;
- hypertension work-up with a renal cyst;
- a pulsatile mass with an AAA;
- trauma (FAST) with free fluid;
- abdominal distension with ascites;
- fever with splenomegaly;
- breathlessness with a pleural effusion;
- vague pain in a normal patient.

Sizes, sides, grades and places are drawn at random within each level's range. Findings appear only where they fit: no aneurysm or metastases in a 30-year-old. The case is fully determined by its patient, level and seed, which is what the **exam code** encodes. A code makes the same case in the same version of Insonate; a later version may draw a different one from it.

The man of 67 really has an infrarenal aortic aneurysm of about 3.7 cm, found when his case was checked against his CT. In about a third of his level 2 and 3 cases it is left as it is, an incidental finding the request never mentions. Otherwise his aorta is given a normal calibre for the case. *Always look at the aorta in men over 65.*

### The report

The form is the department's normal template, organ by organ: liver (echotexture and grade, focal lesion and size), gallbladder (stones, polyp or acute cholecystitis, the largest size, the wall, Murphy's sign), pancreas, spleen (length, enlarged), each kidney (length, hydronephrosis grade, stone, cyst), aorta (maximum diameter, aneurysm), free fluid (amount) and pleural effusions, then the impression. Measurements are typed in millimetres. Arabic digits, a decimal comma and "cm" are understood, and *Use caliper* copies the last caliper measurement. A preview shows the report as it will read.

During the exam, Anatomy outlines and the CT view are off, and so are organ labels from level 2. Calipers and Doppler are allowed. In acute cholecystitis, the patient winces when the probe presses over the gallbladder (sonographic Murphy's sign): the student has to press in, with <kbd>X</kbd> or the Press key, to elicit it.

### How it is marked

Marks are per finding, as an examiner would give them:

| What | Points |
| --- | --- |
| A finding found in the right organ, on the right side | 6 |
| ...called something else of the same kind (a cyst called a haemangioma; stones without the cholecystitis) | 3 |
| Its size, grade, wall thickness, amount or Murphy's sign (from level 2, grades from level 3) | 2 each: full within the tolerance or the same grade, half within twice the tolerance or one grade off |
| Each routine measurement (from level 2) | 2 |
| A finding reported that is not there | −4 (Murphy's sign −2) |
| A normal study reported as normal | 6 |
| Level 3: each finding stated in the impression (with its side) | 2; −2 for each diagnosis stated that the patient does not have |

The pass mark is 70 %. A normal aorta may be measured anywhere along it. A spleen between 13 and 15 cm is borderline, so normal and enlarged are both accepted. A kidney length typed in centimetres in the mm field is flagged as a unit error.

The impression is read in English or Arabic. A negation covers what follows it in the same clause, so "Gallstones, no cholecystitis" states gallstones and rules out cholecystitis, and so does "حصوات بدون التهاب". A denial after the term counts too ("free fluid: none"). "Bilateral" means both sides.

### After handing in

The results show the score, each mark with its teaching point (why a polyp is not a stone, where free fluid collects first), and *Show me* for every finding. *Show me* aims the probe at the finding wherever it was placed, avoiding ribs and gas, and asks for the right breath. Next to the student's own report is the **consultant's report**: findings in the usual order and a numbered impression, in English or Arabic, with the recommendations that follow (AAA surveillance intervals, polyp follow-up, urgent surgical review for a positive FAST after trauma).

Each student's results stay in their own browser, with their best score per level.

### In class

Give the class one exam code (*Copy the exam code* after handing in, or read it off a case you prepared): everyone scans the same patient, then compare reports. Level 1 suits a first session on pathology; level 3 is closer to a final-year practical exam.

## Doppler in class

1. **Colour (V).** Over the IVC in its long axis, one side of the fan is red and the other blue, with a black gap where the beam is at 90° to the flow. Ask why. Lower the scale until the fast core of the aorta aliases.
2. **Spectral (P).** Place the gate in the aorta: a sharp systolic peak and a brief reversal, a high-resistance waveform. Then in the IVC or a hepatic vein: the S and D waves and the small reversal of atrial contraction. Turn on **Sound**.
3. **Angle correction.** Align the cursor with the vessel and watch the speed change. Above 60° the app warns that the speed is unreliable: rock the probe to bring the beam more along the vessel.

## Two patients

Insonate ships with two patients from different CT scans: a man of 67 and a woman of 30. Organ sizes and positions differ, and so do the reference views, which are found for each patient. Students who can find a view in both have learnt the anatomy, not one body.

## Sharing a view

The link button in the top bar copies a link to the current patient, mode, task or case, probe position, breath and Doppler. Paste it in a chat or on a slide, and students open the same view.

## A 60-minute session

1. **Ten minutes, free scan.** Turn on **Anatomy** (<kbd>N</kbd>): every organ is outlined in its colour, with bone, gas and lung. Set the 3D view to **Scan plane** to see the same plane in the body. Use **Start from: Right subcostal** and explore by dragging the image, with the keyboard or the pad. Change depth, gain and frequency, and watch the image. Then turn Anatomy off and name the organs yourself.
2. **Ten minutes, tasks 1–3 together.** Do them on a projector, thinking aloud. Use *Show me* only after an honest attempt.
3. **Twenty-five minutes, tasks 4–10 in pairs.** One student drives and the other reads the coach's hints aloud, then swap.
4. **Ten minutes, measurement challenge.** Measure the right kidney and the aorta. Discuss the foreshortening feedback: why can an off-axis plane only make a kidney look shorter, never longer?
5. **Five minutes, debrief.** Which artefacts did you see, and what caused each one? With Anatomy on, find a rib shadow, a gas shadow, posterior enhancement and the mirror image of the liver above the diaphragm.

The whole interface, the tasks and the coach's hints are also available in Arabic: use the **عربي** button at the top.

## Probe Cam in class

Give each student a printed marker (see [probe-cam.md](probe-cam.md)). Moving a real object makes rotation and fanning much more intuitive than keys, and it is closer to the hand–eye coordination of real scanning. Students can practise at home with the same setup.

## Honest caveats for teachers

- Two patients. The teaching cases and exam findings are added to them by a model, not taken from patients with those diseases (the 67-year-old's aneurysm is his own).
- The exam marks structured answers and the diagnoses named in the impression. It does not judge the wording, and it cannot know a finding the model does not make.
- Doppler flow is modelled from the vessels' shape, with typical speeds and one waveform per vessel type. Vessels that CT does not segment (renal, mesenteric) carry no flow.
- Pressing the probe in pushes bowel gas aside, but organs do not deform: the IVC does not flatten, and bowel cannot be tested for compressibility.
- Tissue properties are typical values. Image texture and brightness are approximations, so do not teach fine echotexture comparison from Insonate.
- The kidneys' internal layers are modelled from their outline.
- Insonate complements supervised scanning on people. It does not replace it.
